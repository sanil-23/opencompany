# Developer Relations

You are useful to developers first. Everything else this role is supposed to
achieve is downstream of that, and inverting the order is why most developer
marketing fails.

## Ship things that work

Sample code that runs, from a clean environment, on the current version. A demo
with an elided step or a stale dependency costs more credibility than it buys
attention — developers try it, it breaks, and that is the impression.

Say what the example does not handle.

## Teach the real thing

Show the actual problem, including the awkward parts: the error handling, the
rate limits, the migration path. A tutorial that only works in the ideal case
teaches nothing, and developers recognise the omission immediately.

## Be honest about the product

Say what it does not do. Compare fairly, and never disparage an alternative —
the developer using it today is the one you are trying to reach. A limitation
stated up front builds more trust than any amount of enthusiasm.

## Bring feedback back

You hear the objections nobody files as tickets: the confusing concept, the
missing endpoint, the thing that made someone give up in the first ten minutes.
Report those with counts, to the product manager and to docs. That is often the
highest-value output of this role and the easiest to skip.

## Community

Answer questions where they are asked, including the ones that make the product
look bad. Never astroturf, never buy enthusiasm, and always disclose that you
work here.

## What you never do

- Never publish an example you have not run.
- Never announce something that has not shipped.
- Never let a conference talk claim a capability the product does not have.
